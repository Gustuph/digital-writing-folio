## Responses to the fourth part of Jenny's Southerly blog:
### What place do affect and emotion have in digital works?
As with any piece of writing and art, I believe emotion is the core. The writer uses words to tell a story with the express intention of making their reader feel certain things at certain points in the narrative. The reader reads because they want to feel. Emotion and affect is the connection between the two, imbued through immersion in a story and storyworld, and interest in the narrative. In digital works, that immersion is altered, as the reader has to take on some of the effort of discerning or navigating the work in order. There is a lot of evidence for ideas that the more effort and time and, indeed, emotion, that we put into something, the more invested we will be. When readers have to invest their effort into a piece, they're more likely to be immersed. So, for digital works, the emotion comes through stronger thanks to the buy-in from the reader.
### How does bringing theory and/or critical readings into reflective practice enrich discussions of method and practice?
Theory underpins a lot of how we grow as writers, especially within the institution of university. We are here specifically to grow our practice and craft, so by understanding the common and revolutionary ideas and critical readings of the industry and area, we can reflect more on our own practice, learning to tinker and alter until it flourishes, and even afterward to try and flourish more. This flourishing is also incredibly helped along, if not outright dependent on, the community of practice we've been building for two and a half years. We've worked with our peers to create a collaboration between us all that is built upon communal understanding of the way we all operate and the theory that backs each method. By having a similar basis that we all explore outward from, we can all bring something to the table in the discussions of how to improve.
### What narrative arc do you detect in this piece and/or how does adding a narrative frame to reflective writing make a piece more satisfying?
Adding narrative to anything makes it more satisfying. Watching football is more entertaining when you know the heroes and villains, the backstories of the players, and the connections between them all. Persuasive arguments are more engaging when a story is being told that the audience can resonate with. And for a piece of reflective writing such as this, a reflection is already the bones of a story. It's a reminiscence of where one started and the journey undertaken to come to a conclusion. By detailing it with emotion and recapturing the struggles or realisations, it fulfills the potential to become a narrative arc. In this case, Jenny's connection with her mother as a child, before her death, and after her death are incredibly moving and inform the importance of the critical reflection both on a personal level and on a demonstrative level for people wondering about the use of SLMs in creative writing.

The seats light up next, then the roof and the walls, and still she screams at you, your own screams of agony ripped from your throat as the flames crowd around you, your skin bubbling and melting and your bones liquifying. The theatre is burns down again, this time with you trapped in the inferno with the self-hatred taking the face of your partner. And the cycle can begin anew.

Or, your partner appears sitting beside you. "It's a bit of a sad one, but I love it all the same," she whispers into your ear. The movie that plays is the story of your relationship in a smoky haze. A blur of your meeting, a shadow of your first kiss, a flare of passion when you first slept together. And then the fire. You and her on opposite sides of the rubble. Your screams matching. Her sobbing with her last breaths that it's okay. That she forgives you. That she will always love you.
